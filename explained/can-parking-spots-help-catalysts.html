Can a catalyst’s “parking spot” make it last longer?

Chemists have long suspected that a catalyst resting in a dormant state can live longer. This paper works out exactly when that is true: only if the catalyst dies by bumping into other catalyst molecules.

This is a preprint: it has been posted for comment but has not yet been peer-reviewed.

The background

A catalyst is a molecule that speeds up a reaction without being used up, at least for a while. Eventually every catalyst dies. The number of product molecules each one makes before that happens is its turnover number, and it decides how much of an expensive metal a process needs.

Catalysts often slip into side states, called off-cycle states, where they rest instead of working. The textbook view is that this is simply bad, because it slows the reaction. Yet several experiments hint that such “parking spots” can protect the catalyst.

What we found

If each catalyst molecule works and dies on its own, a parking spot changes nothing about how many turns it gets. It only slows the reaction, and if something else is ticking down in the background, such as a poison, it lowers the total.

A parking spot helps only when death needs two catalyst molecules to meet, for example when they clump together. Then, while parked, a catalyst cannot meet anyone. A fast parking spot behaves like diluting the solution: the deeper it is, the fewer collisions. The turnover number rises up to a ceiling, and there is a best depth. Park too deeply and the reaction is too slow to finish in the time available.

Applied to published data, a copper hydride catalyst has a parking spot 3 to 6 times deeper than ideal, while in a ruthenium metathesis catalyst the parking spot slows the reaction threefold without costing any turnovers.

Why it matters

The paper gives experimenters a simple test. Run the reaction at lower catalyst loading: if each catalyst molecule then makes more product, a parking spot can help. If not, it cannot, and chemists can save themselves the effort.
