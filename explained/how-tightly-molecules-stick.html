How tightly does a catalyst molecule stick to a surface? The usual shortcut gets it badly wrong

The standard way of calculating how strongly a molecule sticks to a surface assumes it sits still. For a rhenium catalyst on graphene, that assumption misses more than half of the answer, because the molecule slides, spins and rocks.

This is a preprint: it has been posted for comment but has not yet been peer-reviewed.

The background

Many modern catalysts are single molecules attached to a solid surface. How strongly a molecule sticks affects how well the catalyst works, so chemists calculate it. The standard shortcut, called the harmonic approximation, treats the stuck molecule like a ball held in a bowl: it can only jiggle a little around one spot.

When such calculations disagree with reality, the usual suspect is that the jiggling is not perfectly regular. I tested that explanation.

What we found

I used a simulation technique called metadynamics, which gradually fills in the valleys of the energy landscape so that the molecule is pushed to explore. The test case was a rhenium complex on graphene at room temperature.

The shortcut gave a binding strength of −26 kJ/mol. The full simulation gave −64 kJ/mol, so the molecule sticks much more strongly than the shortcut says. Of the 38 kJ/mol gap, only about 2 comes from the irregular jiggling people usually blame. The other 36 come from something else: the stuck molecule is not held in a bowl at all. It can slide across the surface, spin around and rock, and that freedom makes sticking more favourable.

The shortcut is still fine for comparing two similar poses of the same molecule, because the missing term cancels. It breaks down when you compare a surface-bound molecule with the same molecule in the gas.

Why it matters

Computed binding strengths feed into predictions of how well a surface-attached catalyst will work. Knowing where the error really comes from tells researchers when the quick method can be trusted and when the longer simulation is worth the cost.
