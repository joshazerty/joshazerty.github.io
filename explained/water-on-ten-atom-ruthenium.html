What does water do on a catalyst only ten atoms wide?

Calculations on a flat ruthenium surface say that water is torn completely apart. Experiments say otherwise. On a realistic ten-atom cluster the experiments win: water stays as OH, and the action is at the cluster’s edge.

The background

Ruthenium particles on titanium dioxide are among the best catalysts for turning oxygen-rich, plant-derived molecules into fuels and chemicals, and they work in hot water. Experiments agree that surface OH groups (an oxygen with one hydrogen) play a central role.

The trouble is that the standard calculation for water on a flat ruthenium surface says no OH should survive at these temperatures: water is predicted to split completely into separate oxygen and hydrogen atoms. Either the experiments or the flat-surface model had to be wrong.

What we found

A real catalyst is not a flat sheet. We modelled a cluster of just ten ruthenium atoms, less than a nanometre across, on titanium dioxide, with between zero and nine water molecules, and sampled a large number of arrangements. OH comes back as the main oxygen species (45 to 50%), sitting on single ruthenium atoms at the cluster’s edge. These edge atoms are poorly surrounded by neighbours and have no counterpart on a flat surface.

Across the temperatures where such catalysts work (about 400–530 K), the predicted working state has around six water molecules and plenty of OH. We also saw hydrogen atoms hop from the metal onto the support. This was more likely when the support had missing oxygen atoms, but it is rare in the working state.

Why it matters

The active site is the edge of the cluster, and the relevant species is OH. That is the picture experiments pointed to, and it gives designers of these catalysts the right starting point.
