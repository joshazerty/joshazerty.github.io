In plain language · Organic synthesis · Medicinal chemistry

A simple rule for building new drug-like molecules, and what they do to cells

A basic quantum-chemical calculation explains why iodine attaches at one particular position on a family of ring-shaped molecules. That position acts as a handle for making three families of compounds, which were then tested as potential drug scaffolds.

The background

Many pharmaceuticals are built on flat ring systems. Variations are made by attaching new groups at specific positions on the ring, which requires a reliable handle on the right atom.

The frameworks in this paper are quinoxalines fused to another ring. They are of interest as kinase inhibitors. Kinases are enzymes that act as on/off switches inside cells and are involved in a number of diseases, including cancer.

What we found

Across a broad range of quinoxalines carrying an amine group, iodine consistently attaches at the position next to the amine. A straightforward calculation using Hückel theory, which identifies where electrons are most concentrated, correctly predicts this position, so the outcome can be explained and anticipated.

The iodine handle was then used to make three families of fused-ring molecules, including the pyrazino-isatins, which were prepared for the first time in this study. Several of the compounds are fluorescent. Screening against enzymes and cancer cells identified new inhibitors of the kinase GSK3 and a selective inhibitor of another kinase, CK1, with sub-micromolar potency.

Why it matters

A rule that predicts where a reaction will happen saves synthetic effort, and here it helped open up new molecular scaffolds with promising biological activity. These compounds are starting points for drug discovery rather than drugs themselves.
