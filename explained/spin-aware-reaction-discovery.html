Teaching a reaction-discovery program about electron spin

Automated programs that map out chemical reactions usually ignore electron spin. I extended an open-source program so that it notices when a reaction can hop between spin states, and so that it warns you when it cannot be trusted.

This is a preprint: it has been posted for comment but has not yet been peer-reviewed.

The background

Programs such as AutoMeKin explore a reaction automatically: they push a molecule around on the computer and record every route it finds, without a chemist choosing the paths in advance.

These programs normally assume that the electrons stay in one arrangement. Iron and other common metals break that assumption. Their electrons can line up in different ways, called spin states, that are close in energy. A reaction can switch from one to another part-way through, a bit like changing floors of a building at a staircase. Sometimes that switch is the bottleneck, and a program that ignores it gives the wrong answer.

What we found

The extension finds spin states that lie close in energy, locates the “staircase” where two spin states cross (the minimum-energy crossing point), and estimates how fast molecules hop across. It also carries a built-in warning: a quick check on the electrons that tells the program when its basic method is too simple to be believed.

I tested it on three iron systems, and each ended in a different way. For an iron–ethyl complex, the crossing was found automatically and shown not to limit the speed, in line with earlier manual studies. For an iron catalyst that shuffles double bonds, the crossing was located but its energy could not be pinned down. For an iron–nitrene system, the program correctly flagged that it could not be treated by the simple method.

Why it matters

A tool that says “this answer is reliable”, “this one is uncertain” and “do not trust this one” is much more useful than one that always returns a number. The code is open source, so others can use it on their own metal catalysts.
