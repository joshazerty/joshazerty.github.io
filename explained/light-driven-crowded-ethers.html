Using light to build crowded ethers

A new reaction uses blue light to build ethers in which the carbon next to oxygen is crowded with bulky groups, something that is difficult to do by conventional chemistry.

This is a preprint: it has been posted for comment but has not yet been peer-reviewed.

The background

Ethers, in which an oxygen atom links two carbon groups, are found in many medicines and materials. When one of those groups is bulky, the ether is hard to make because the pieces get in each other’s way, and traditional methods tend to fail.

Chemists increasingly use light to make highly reactive fragments called radicals, which can form bonds that ordinary reactions cannot.

What we found

The team showed that visible light, a cheap phosphorus reagent and a radical source can attach bulky groups to the oxygen of a two-carbonyl molecule (a 1,2-diketone). Many bulky groups and many diketones worked. The reaction also worked at a 2 mmol scale (68% yield), and adapting it to a flow reactor, where the mixture runs through a narrow, transparent tube, made it faster and more efficient in most cases.

My part was computational. A radical meeting a carbonyl could in principle attach to carbon or to oxygen. The calculations show that attaching to oxygen is easier and leads to a stable intermediate, while attaching to carbon is harder and easily reverses. The margin is large for bulky radicals and disappears for smaller ones, which explains why only bulky radicals work. The calculations also show that the light-driven electron-transfer steps are feasible with blue light, and why diketones carrying electron-donating groups react less well.

Why it matters

Crowded ethers are valuable building blocks, and the flow version of the reaction shows a route towards larger-scale production. The calculations explain the reaction’s limits instead of leaving them as trial and error.
