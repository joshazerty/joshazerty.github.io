What happens when a “forever chemical” fragment lands on ice?

Hexafluoroacetone, a breakdown product of PFAS “forever chemicals”, does not stay on the surface of ice. Instead, it diffuses into the ice and reacts completely with water to form a compound called a diol, which is the form that matters in the environment.

The background

PFAS are synthetic chemicals that persist in the environment and are now ubiquitous. Larger PFAS compounds can degrade into smaller molecules, such as hexafluoroacetone (HFA), which can enter the atmosphere.

In the atmosphere, ice particles in clouds can capture these molecules. How HFA behaves in the environment depends on whether it simply sticks to the ice or reacts with it.

What we found

In the experiments, thin films of ice were grown on a platinum crystal in a vacuum chamber, dosed with HFA and heated gradually while monitoring what left the surface. X-ray measurements identified the products. The results show that HFA diffuses into the ice and reacts completely with water to form a diol, in which a water molecule is chemically bonded to HFA.

The energies needed to remove each species from the surface are 44 kJ/mol for HFA from platinum, 52 kJ/mol for the ice itself and 62 kJ/mol for the diol. Calculations show that forming the diol on ice is favoured by at least 29 kJ/mol over leaving HFA unreacted. On heating, the ice starts to evaporate before the diol is released.

Why it matters

To predict the atmospheric fate of this PFAS fragment, it should be treated as the diol on ice rather than as HFA. This distinction influences how it is transported and what it may react with.
