Why a graphite support helps a bio-inspired catalyst turn CO2 into fuel

A nickel–iron catalyst modelled on a natural enzyme can convert CO2 to methane when supported on graphite. Computational results indicate that graphite facilitates electron transfer and enhances CO2 binding.

The background

Electrochemical conversion of CO2 into fuel offers a way to store renewable energy and to use up a greenhouse gas. Natural enzymes containing nickel and iron process small gas molecules, and chemists have synthesised simpler molecules that mimic their active sites.

A nickel–iron complex was observed to convert CO2 to methane when attached to graphite, a form of carbon. The graphite support was not merely a physical holder, and its precise role remained unclear.

What we found

Quantum chemical calculations were performed on a model of the complex supported on a graphene sheet. Initial tests confirmed that a small graphene fragment behaves similarly to a larger sheet.

Attachment to graphene provides two key benefits. Electrons move efficiently between the graphene and the complex, governed by the complex’s electronic state. In addition, the energy cost of inserting CO2 into the complex is reduced.

Why it matters

The support functions as an integral component of the catalyst. Understanding its role informs the choice of carbon materials for catalysts designed to convert CO2 into fuels.
