Missing oxygen atoms reshape tiny metal catalysts

Real catalyst supports are never perfect: they have missing atoms. Calculations show that these gaps sit under a ten-atom ruthenium cluster and change its shape, from flat towards rounded.

The background

Tiny metal particles on an oxide, such as ruthenium on titanium dioxide, are workhorse catalysts. The oxide is more than a stand: its surface can lack some oxygen atoms, leaving gaps called oxygen vacancies, which are common in real materials and change how electrons move around.

The shape of the metal particle matters, because it decides which atoms are exposed to the molecules being transformed. So the question is how those gaps change the shape.

What we found

We computed the energy of many shapes of a ten-atom ruthenium cluster, with and without a missing oxygen in the support. The gaps prefer to be directly beneath the cluster. When they are there, the cluster touches the oxide less and shifts from flattened towards more rounded shapes.

We also found stable shapes with ruthenium atoms that have few neighbours, which could be especially reactive. The most stable shape is not automatically the most catalytically active one.

Why it matters

Catalyst performance can depend on defects that are easy to overlook. Models of real catalysts should include them, and this work provides a map of how they influence the particle on top.
