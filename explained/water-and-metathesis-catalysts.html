Even a drop of water can ruin a “water-tolerant” catalyst

Ruthenium catalysts for olefin metathesis were previously considered water tolerant. This study shows that even trace amounts of water significantly reduce their activity, and identifies the most water-tolerant catalysts.

The background

Olefin metathesis is a process in which the partners on carbon–carbon double bonds are exchanged, much like dancers changing partners. It is used to make pharmaceuticals, polymers and fragrances, and was honoured with the 2005 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Leading ruthenium catalysts are known for their water tolerance and are even used in water to modify proteins. However, these demanding applications typically need large quantities of catalyst, and the effect of small amounts of water in ordinary solvents, where water is a common contaminant, had not been systematically investigated.

What we found

Eleven catalysts were evaluated. In a ring-forming reaction that produces a fragrance molecule, 0.01% water in the solvent reduced the yield obtained with the first catalyst by approximately 60%. At 0.1–1% water, the reaction essentially failed. In a separate test reaction, two catalysts achieved only 600 and 1,800 turnovers with 1% water, compared with approximately 18,000 turnovers under dry conditions.

The catalysts that start working fastest lost the most performance, likely because they remain in the active, and therefore more vulnerable, form for longer. In contrast, the iodide and CAAC catalysts, which initiate more slowly, were more resilient, and the most robust retained 56% of its dry performance. Computational analysis suggested an additional effect: water hydrogen-bonding to the starting material can slow the desired ring closure, which favours decomposition.

Why it matters

Water is present in virtually every laboratory, so it is easy to assume that a catalyst is water tolerant, and that assumption can quietly cost yield. The results help explain why some reactions were hard to reproduce and highlight catalyst features that improve water tolerance. They are particularly relevant for chemistry carried out in water-rich environments.
