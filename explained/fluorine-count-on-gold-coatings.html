More fluorine, same effect: what a molecular coating on gold really responds to

Increasing the number of fluorine atoms in a molecular coating on gold might be expected to change the surface properties, but no such effect is observed. This is attributed to the coating lying flat and bonding strongly to the gold, which overrides the influence of the extra fluorine atoms.

The background

Single molecular layers can be used to change how strongly a surface repels water or how easily electrons escape from it. Fluorine atoms are often included in these coatings because they are highly electronegative, meaning they pull electrons strongly. However, traditional long-chain fluorinated compounds are toxic and are being phased out, so alternative materials are needed.

A good anchor is the N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC), a carbon-based group that binds gold very well. NHCs allow the fluorine atoms to sit extremely close to the metal, which was difficult to achieve before. This makes it possible to ask directly how much the number of fluorine atoms matters.

What we found

Seven fluorinated NHCs, containing one to five fluorine atoms, were synthesised and applied to gold surfaces. Each formed a film that lay flat on the surface and was attached through a gold atom pulled out of the surface. Increased fluorine content on the ring enhanced adhesion to gold but also displaced the carbon anchor from its optimal position, thereby weakening the anchoring interaction.

The coatings lowered the work function of gold (the energy needed to pull an electron out of the surface) by up to 1 eV and increased the water contact angle by 15 to 20 degrees, meaning that water beads up more. However, these effects did not depend on the number of fluorine atoms.

Why it matters

These findings carry a lesson for coating design: changing a molecule’s side groups does not necessarily change the surface properties. How the molecule sits on, and interacts with, the metal may matter more than what the side groups are.
