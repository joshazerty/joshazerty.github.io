Why a pinch of acid helps turn plant-derived chemicals into useful ones

Adding a little acid makes a cheap rhenium catalyst work far better. We found, on the computer, exactly where the acid acts: it helps a stuck oxygen–hydrogen group leave as water.

The background

Plants are rich in molecules that carry many oxygen atoms. To turn them into fuels and plastics, chemists first have to remove some of that oxygen. One reaction that does this is called deoxydehydration: a molecule with two neighbouring OH groups loses them as water and gains a carbon–carbon double bond.

The cheapest and sturdiest catalyst for this job is perrhenate, a simple rhenium–oxygen ion. For twenty years, experiments have shown that acid makes it work much faster, in one case turning a reaction that did not run at all into one that finished in a couple of hours. Nobody knew where the acid was acting.

What we found

We built the mechanism step by step on the computer. The key is that, in the catalyst, an OH group sits on rhenium and is reluctant to leave. A proton from the acid turns it into a water molecule, which drifts away happily. That single move unlocks the two steps that follow: closing the ring around the diol, and the step in which the alcohol, acting as the reducing agent, takes oxygen away from rhenium.

In our model this makes the reaction roughly a hundred million times faster than the best acid-free route we could find. The overall speed predicted by the model also falls inside the range implied by published experiments.

Finding the peak of each hill, the transition state, is slow work. We chose the routes to test; an AI agent then carried out the searches for all 44 transition states, and we checked each result by hand.

Why it matters

The model gives a practical prediction: the amount of acid should match the amount of rhenium, and removing the water and ketone by-products should speed the reaction further. It also shows that AI-assisted searches can map out a full catalytic cycle.
