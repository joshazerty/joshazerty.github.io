Research directions

Catalysis under operating conditions

Put a supported catalyst under reaction conditions and it restructures, changes oxidation state, picks up hydroxyls and water, and sometimes dissolves into the medium that was meant to be the reactant. So I start by working out which species are populated at a given temperature, pressure and solvent activity, then ask what those species do. The solvent is in that calculation from the start.

  • ab initio thermodynamics
  • global optimisation
  • metadynamics
  • QM/MM FEP

Automated reaction mechanism discovery

Mechanistic studies fail more often on a step nobody thought to draw than on a badly computed barrier, and homogeneous catalysis is where that bites hardest. Automated exploration enumerates the candidate steps, verifies the transition states, and lets the kinetics pick the route that carries the flux.

An LLM agent driving those tools can propose chemistry and run the calculation that would refute it. Proposing is the easy half. I spend the effort on verification, so you can check what the agent reports instead of trusting it.

  • agentic AI discovery
  • reaction network exploration
  • homogeneous catalysis
  • IRC verification

Molecules on surfaces

Put an ordered organic layer on a metal and the two stop behaving like separate things: the adsorbate shifts the work function, the metal shifts the adsorbate. With Elad Gross's group in Jerusalem I have worked on fluorinated N-heterocyclic carbene and carbodicarbene monolayers on gold, computing what the measured surface properties imply about the binding.

The current strand, with the De Feyter group here in Leuven, is scanning tunnelling microscopy. An STM image is not a structure, and getting from one to the other means modelling the adlayer and the contrast it should produce.

  • STM modelling
  • self-assembled monolayers
  • NHC and CDC ligands
  • work-function shifts

Photochemistry and excited states

Excited-state chemistry runs on a different set of rules: the molecule absorbs, lands on an upper surface, and comes back down wherever the surfaces touch. Predicting the product means finding those crossings.

I started on this at Durham with Basile Curchod, mapping where TD-DFT stops being trustworthy for the excited states of atmospherically relevant molecules. Knowing the limits of the method matters more here than almost anywhere else, because a bad excited state still looks like a converged calculation.

  • TD-DFT
  • conical intersections
  • nonadiabatic dynamics
  • atmospheric photochemistry

Chirality-induced spin selectivity

Send electrons through a film of chiral molecules and they come out spin-polarised, with the handedness of the molecule deciding which spin survives. The effect holds at room temperature, and that is the puzzle: spin-orbit coupling in light-element organics falls orders of magnitude short of explaining polarisations this large.

Different groups blame the metal contact, the vibrations or the electron correlation, and the field has not settled. I come at it from the interface, where my surface work already sits, because the chiral layer and the metal underneath it behave as a single system and the calculation has to treat them that way.

  • CISS
  • spin-orbit coupling
  • chiral monolayers
  • spin transport

Machine-learned potentials for transition metals

Sampling at DFT cost is what limits everything above, and foundation interatomic potentials come close to lifting that limit. They break down on open-shell transition-metal chemistry, and most of them carry no notion of spin state, so a model that handles organic reactions well can walk straight past the surface crossing that sets the rate. I measure where they fail, fine-tune around it, and publish the benchmarks.

  • MACE
  • UMA
  • fine-tuning
  • spin-state benchmarks

Kinetics that can be tested

An experimentalist can measure a turnover frequency, an apparent activation energy, a reaction order or a kinetic isotope effect. None of them can measure a free-energy diagram. Deriving the first set from the second often shows that the tallest barrier is not the step in control. I build micro-kinetic models on computed networks and push the uncertainty in the barriers through to the observable, so you can see how much the comparison with experiment settles.

  • micro-kinetic modelling
  • degree of rate control
  • energetic span
  • kinetic isotope effects
