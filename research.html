Research

Catalysis happens at interfaces that are messy, dynamic and solvated. These projects are attempts to compute them as they are, rather than as the tractable idealisation.

Speciation and morphology of supported Ru

Sub-nanometre ruthenium clusters on titania do not keep a fixed shape. Whether a Ru10 cluster wets the support or balls up depends on the reducing power of the surface underneath it, and oxygen vacancies in the anatase change that balance enough to switch the preferred morphology.

The consequence matters for catalysis: shape sets the fraction of under-coordinated Ru atoms, which is what the reaction actually sees. This was the core of my doctoral work with Carine Michel, built on global optimisation over a large configuration space followed by periodic DFT, with an explicit water phase added on top.

Solvation and free energies at oxide interfaces

An adsorption energy computed in vacuum is often the wrong number by more than the effect being studied. Getting the solvated value instead means sampling, and sampling at the DFT level is expensive enough that the method matters as much as the model.

I have worked on both ends of this. On the method side, resampling estimators that carry a cheap force field trajectory up to a DFT or machine-learned surface, developed with Stephan Steinmann. On the application side, metadynamics for alcohol adsorption on alumina, and explicit-water models of the titania interface. A related strand concerns catalyst deactivation: trace water is enough to quench ruthenium olefin-metathesis catalysts, and the mechanism by which it does so is a solvation problem as much as a mechanistic one.

Small molecules on ice

Atmospheric chemistry on ice surfaces is a case where computation and experiment can be pinned tightly together. Working with Javier Giorgi's group in Ottawa, we looked at how hexafluoroacetone hydrates on an ice surface, combining surface-science measurements with calculations that explain which hydrated form is present and why.

An earlier strand, at Durham with Basile Curchod, probed where TD-DFT stops being trustworthy for the excited states of atmospherically relevant molecules. Both projects share a concern with knowing the limits of the method before trusting the answer.

Mechanism, kinetics and machine-learned potentials

My current work at KU Leuven sits on the mechanistic side: locating transition states reliably, turning computed networks into micro-kinetic models, and asking which step actually controls the rate rather than which barrier is tallest. Part of this runs within an iBOF consortium on the catalytic valorisation of biomass-derived polyols, where rhenium-oxo chemistry raises the awkward question of what the working catalyst is.

Doing any of that with enough sampling requires potentials faster than DFT. Foundation models for interatomic potentials are close to good enough, but open-shell transition-metal chemistry is exactly where they are weakest, and spin states are handled poorly or not at all. I am working on characterising those failures honestly and fine-tuning around them, with the benchmarks released rather than kept in-house.
