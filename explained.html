Papers in plain language

Short explanations of each of my papers for readers with an interest in science but no specialist background: the problem, the key result and why it matters. Each links to the full paper.

Full publication list

2026

Why a pinch of acid helps turn plant-derived chemicals into useful ones

Adding a little acid makes a cheap rhenium catalyst work far better. We found, on the computer, exactly where the acid acts: it helps a stuck oxygen–hydrogen group leave as water.

Chemical Science Read the summary 10.1039/D6SC06865K

Teaching a reaction-discovery program about electron spin

Automated programs that map out chemical reactions usually ignore electron spin. I extended an open-source program so that it notices when a reaction can hop between spin states, and so that it warns you when it cannot be trusted.

ChemRxiv Preprint Read the summary 10.26434/chemrxiv.15010937/v1

How tightly does a catalyst molecule stick to a surface? The usual shortcut gets it badly wrong

The standard way of calculating how strongly a molecule sticks to a surface assumes it sits still. For a rhenium catalyst on graphene, that assumption misses more than half of the answer, because the molecule slides, spins and rocks.

ChemRxiv Preprint Read the summary 10.26434/chemrxiv.15010935/v1

Can a catalyst’s “parking spot” make it last longer?

Chemists have long suspected that a catalyst resting in a dormant state can live longer. This paper works out exactly when that is true: only if the catalyst dies by bumping into other catalyst molecules.

ChemRxiv Preprint Read the summary 10.26434/chemrxiv.15010890/v1

Using light to build crowded ethers

A new reaction uses blue light to build ethers in which the carbon next to oxygen is crowded with bulky groups, something that is difficult to do by conventional chemistry.

ChemRxiv Preprint Read the summary 10.26434/chemrxiv.15006666/v2

A new molecular coating that changes the electronic “skin” of gold

A little-used type of molecule, built around a very electron-rich carbon atom, grips gold tightly, survives heating, and makes it easier for gold to release electrons.

Small Read the summary 10.1002/smll.76364

What does water do on a catalyst only ten atoms wide?

Calculations on a flat ruthenium surface say that water is torn completely apart. Experiments say otherwise. On a realistic ten-atom cluster the experiments win: water stays as OH, and the action is at the cluster’s edge.

Applied Surface Science Read the summary 10.1016/j.apsusc.2026.168653

2025

Missing oxygen atoms reshape tiny metal catalysts

Real catalyst supports are never perfect: they have missing atoms. Calculations show that these gaps sit under a ten-atom ruthenium cluster and change its shape, from flat towards rounded.

Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics Read the summary 10.1039/D5CP01579K

What happens when a “forever chemical” fragment lands on ice?

Hexafluoroacetone, a breakdown product of PFAS “forever chemicals”, does not stay on the surface of ice. Instead, it diffuses into the ice and reacts completely with water to form a compound called a diol, which is the form that matters in the environment.

Journal of Physical Chemistry C Read the summary 10.1021/acs.jpcc.5c00560

2024

More fluorine, same effect: what a molecular coating on gold really responds to

Increasing the number of fluorine atoms in a molecular coating on gold might be expected to change the surface properties, but no such effect is observed. This is attributed to the coating lying flat and bonding strongly to the gold, which overrides the influence of the extra fluorine atoms.

ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces Read the summary 10.1021/acsami.4c12514

Why a graphite support helps a bio-inspired catalyst turn CO2 into fuel

A nickel–iron catalyst modelled on a natural enzyme can convert CO2 to methane when supported on graphite. Computational results indicate that graphite facilitates electron transfer and enhances CO2 binding.

Journal of Computational Chemistry Read the summary 10.1002/jcc.27355

2021

Even a drop of water can ruin a “water-tolerant” catalyst

Ruthenium catalysts for olefin metathesis were previously considered water tolerant. This study shows that even trace amounts of water significantly reduce their activity, and identifies the most water-tolerant catalysts.

ACS Catalysis Read the summary 10.1021/acscatal.0c04279

A simple rule for building new drug-like molecules, and what they do to cells

A basic quantum-chemical calculation explains why iodine attaches at one particular position on a family of ring-shaped molecules. That position acts as a handle for making three families of compounds, which were then tested as potential drug scaffolds.

European Journal of Organic Chemistry Read the summary 10.1002/ejoc.202100362
